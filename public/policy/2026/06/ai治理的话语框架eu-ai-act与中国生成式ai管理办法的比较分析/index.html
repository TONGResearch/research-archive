AI治理的话语框架：EU AI Act与中国《生成式AI管理办法》的比较分析

A Comparative Discourse Analysis of AI Governance Frameworks

2026-06-03 · draft · AIEUChinaregulationdiscourse-analysis · ai-governancecomparative-policy

摘要 / Abstract

This memo compares the discursive construction of “trustworthy AI” in two major regulatory frameworks: the EU AI Act (2024) and China’s Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative AI Services (2023). Drawing on Foucauldian discourse analysis, I argue that both frameworks mobilize the concept of trustworthiness differently — the EU anchors it in fundamental rights and liability, while China anchors it in social stability and Party-state oversight. These are not merely technical differences; they reflect distinct political rationalities.

1. Introduction

The global race to regulate AI has produced a proliferation of governance documents, white papers, and regulatory frameworks. Rather than treating these as neutral technical standards, this memo reads them as political texts — discursive constructions that reveal the assumptions, interests, and power relations embedded in each governance tradition.

The central question: Who defines “trustworthy AI,” and in whose interest?

2. The EU Framework: Rights-Based Trustworthiness

The EU AI Act organizes trustworthiness around three pillars:

  1. Fundamental rights protection — AI systems must not violate rights guaranteed by the EU Charter
  2. Risk classification — systems are tiered by potential harm, with highest-risk systems subject to mandatory conformity assessment
  3. Liability and accountability — clear chains of responsibility from provider to deployer

The implicit subject of this framework is the individual citizen whose rights must be protected from algorithmic harm. Trustworthiness is a legal and procedural category.

3. The Chinese Framework: Stability-Based Trustworthiness

The Interim Measures (生成式人工智能服务管理暂行办法) constructs trustworthiness differently:

  1. Social stability — generated content must not “endanger national security or social stability”
  2. Socialist core values — content must reflect and promote these values
  3. State-platform co-governance — platforms bear primary enforcement responsibility

The implicit subject here is the social body (社会整体) whose cohesion must be maintained. Trustworthiness is a political and moral category.

5. Provisional Conclusions

Neither framework is politically neutral. Both encode specific visions of the relationship between technology, state, and society. The EU framework reflects a liberal rights tradition; the Chinese framework reflects a developmental state tradition with socialist characteristics.

For comparative governance research, the key implication is that technical standards (safety, reliability, transparency) are always also political standards — and their cross-border translation is a site of geopolitical contestation, not merely technical harmonization.

6. Open Questions

  • How do middle-power states (India, Singapore, UAE) navigate between these two frameworks?
  • Is there a possible third model that is neither liberal-rights nor Party-state centered?
  • What role do Chinese tech companies play in shaping the domestic framework?
