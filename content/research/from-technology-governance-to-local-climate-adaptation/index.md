In the summer of 2026, I attended the Global Public Policy Summer School at Fudan University in Shanghai, studying technology governance and public policy with Professor Dan Guttman. The programme was jointly organised by Fudan University's Institute for Global Public Policy and the Fudan-LSE Research Centre for Global Public Policy.

My initial interest was AI governance. As a university student majoring in computer science, I wanted to understand how societies should establish rules as technical capabilities advance, and what problems technology can actually help public institutions solve.

One important change I took away from the course was a greater insistence on specificity: which technology are we discussing, whom does it serve, which decision should it support, and who must take responsibility for its consequences?

## A Course About Technology and Institutions

The course covered much more than climate governance. It moved from the scientific and industrial revolutions, the Cold War, and nuclear technology to technology assessment, climate change, energy transitions, geoengineering, biotechnology, and cyber and AI governance.

These topics share a recurring problem. Technology can create substantial benefits, but it can also cause harms that are difficult to anticipate or reverse. Technical knowledge helps identify possible consequences. Responding to them also requires organisations, authority, resources, and decisions about different groups' interests.

I became particularly interested in institutional settings and the rules of the game. Governments, businesses, professional organisations, and informal networks can all influence the same technology. Looking only for a law or a regulator may miss relationships that actually determine what happens.

For a computer science student, this also changes the boundary of the system under study. A program's inputs and outputs are only part of the process. Who supplies the data, who interprets the output, and who corrects errors can also change its real-world performance.

## Why My Research Moved Towards Local Adaptation

I originally wanted to compare climate governance and AI governance. That remains a meaningful direction, but my initial framing was too broad to make clear what the comparison would explain.

Professor Guttman's emphasis on local climate adaptation offered a more specific entry point. Mitigation concerns reducing greenhouse gas emissions or enhancing sinks; adaptation concerns responding to actual or expected climate impacts. They are related, but implementing adaptation requires knowing where risks occur, who is affected, and what action is locally possible.

My course paper consequently focused on the subdistrict level in Xinghualing District, Taiyuan, and took the form of a policy memo for a local policy implementer. This was my first formal course paper. It introduced an important writing constraint: the intended reader needed analysis that could help them understand risks and organise work, rather than a comprehensive discussion of AI's future.

## Examining How a Question Is Constructed

During this process, I realised that asking whether AI can predict climate risk still leaves several questions unresolved.

Climate models, weather forecasts, local risk assessments, and generative AI responses are not interchangeable. Organising information is not the same as building a predictive model. Summarising city-level research does not produce a reliable estimate for a particular street.

Nor does identifying a risk determine the response. Even if a location may flood, someone must establish whether the information is current, whether the route is accessible, whether staff are available, and who can change the work arrangements.

I therefore began focusing on the process through which information becomes usable: how public materials and model outputs enter local work, how local knowledge corrects omissions, and how staff explain, verify, and use that information.

## Where Computer Science Can Contribute

Interdisciplinary study does not require leaving computer science behind. Data structures, model evaluation, access control, and system design can help me express governance problems more precisely.

For example, should a system retain only a risk score, or also a report's time, source, review status, and conflicting observations? When a model disagrees with field observations, can staff pause its recommendation and record why it was changed? These are technical design choices, but also arrangements concerning knowledge and responsibility.

I have not built a validated local decision support system. At this stage, the more important task is to formulate testable questions, identify missing evidence, and avoid mistaking a convincing interface for a solution to a governance problem.

## Questions I Want to Pursue

This experience helped me identify a developing research thread: **under what conditions can information produced by computational systems support better public judgment?**

That question extends to the relationship between predictive accuracy and decision outcomes, the representation of local knowledge, and the rules for human intervention. Each direction requires technical analysis as well as an understanding of the people and organisations using the technology.

This archive is intended to preserve that process: how questions become more focused, which judgments gain support, which proposals need revision, and what evidence should come next.
