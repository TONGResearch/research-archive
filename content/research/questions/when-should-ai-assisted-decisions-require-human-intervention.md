question_description: >-
  Saying that a human remains involved does not specify when that person must act or
  whether they can meaningfully change an outcome. This question investigates which
  conditions should require an AI-assisted process to pause, seek authorisation, or move
  to a safer alternative. Possible triggers include consequences for basic rights,
  potentially irreversible harm, unfamiliar operating conditions, conflicting evidence,
  and objections from affected people. It connects my interest in human judgement and
  Slow AI with the practical design of decision-support and agentic systems.
scope: >-
  The initial focus is public-sector decision support and local climate adaptation,
  with agricultural agent systems as a possible comparative case. Human-in-the-loop
  and human-on-the-loop arrangements will be examined as different allocations of
  involvement, not a universal ranking of better and worse oversight. The scope includes
  escalation rules, permissions, review time, explanation, human override, logging,
  and safe fallback procedures.
objectives: >-
  To outline a context-sensitive framework for intervention triggers; distinguish decisions
  that require prior human authorisation from those suitable for bounded autonomy;
  and specify what information, time, and authority reviewers need. Possible testing
  through scenarios or a small prototype would examine missed escalations, excessive
  alerts, response delays, and whether a reviewer can actually stop or change an action.
current_position: >-
  My provisional position is that meaningful oversight requires a person who can understand
  the issue, has time to respond, and has the authority to alter or halt the process.
  A confidence score or a final approval button is not enough on its own. Decisions with
  serious or irreversible consequences may require review before action, while lower-risk,
  reversible actions may permit bounded autonomy. If effective review is unavailable,
  the system should have a defined safe fallback rather than silently treating that absence
  as approval.
