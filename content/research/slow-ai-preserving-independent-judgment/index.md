At the earlier online forum on AI in education, Dr Shabbar introduced the idea of Slow AI. In *When Answers Are No Longer Scarce*, I recorded my understanding: preserve necessary independent thinking before consulting AI, then exercise judgment and verification after receiving its output.

The origin matters. I did not coin the term, and I do not present it here as a validated educational intervention. What I want to develop is its relevance to my own research and writing process.

Following the summer course and my local adaptation paper, a more practical question became important: how can independent thinking actually occur within the workflow, rather than appear only as a reminder at the end of an essay?

## Record an Initial Judgment Before Seeing an Answer

For tasks involving analysis and trade-offs, I would like to begin with a short record: what the problem is, how I currently understand it, what evidence I have, and what I do not yet know.

The record need not be complete or correct. Its purpose is to make it possible, after reading an AI response, to distinguish prior understanding from changes suggested later.

Without that step, I may quickly acquire a well-structured answer while struggling to explain why I accepted its framing.

## An Example From Local Adaptation

Asked how AI could help a city respond to climate change, a system can readily generate a long list covering energy, transport, buildings, and warnings.

Before asking, I could narrow the task: the intended reader is a subdistrict policy implementer; the concern is how local flooding reports enter inspection arrangements; long-term adaptation planning must be distinguished from procedures during a particular event.

The question then becomes how, given an official warning, local reports could be organised, missing evidence identified, and items prepared for staff verification.

This question is not necessarily easier to answer, but the answer's relevance is easier to check. It also requires me to understand the user's work before delegating the framing entirely to AI.

## Use AI to Compare and Challenge

After forming an initial judgment, I would like AI to help find omissions, not merely expand my position into a more persuasive argument.

It can be asked for alternative explanations, conclusions needing additional evidence, or conditions under which a recommendation would fail. Even a seemingly settled question can be reframed to examine the object of study.

These outputs remain material for verification. Similar answers from several systems do not automatically constitute independent validation: the systems may rely on similar sources or repeat the same mistake.

## Verification Must Return to Evidence

When an output names a local policy, a precise figure, or an operating platform, I need traceable original material. If none can be found, the statement should remain an unconfirmed lead, not enter the essay simply because it fits my expectations.

I also want to distinguish verifiable facts, conditional inferences, and my own value judgments or recommendations. They require different kinds of support.

This is particularly important for generated local analysis. The more specific the language, the more closely I need to examine whether the evidence is equally specific.

## A Minimal Research Record

I would like to try recording important human-AI tasks in the following way. It is a proposed method, not a claim that I have consistently followed a complete workflow in all my research.

| Record | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Original question | What am I trying to understand or decide? |
| Independent initial judgment | How did I analyse it before seeing an AI response? |
| AI's contribution | What did it add, challenge, or change? |
| Critical verification | Which sources supported or contradicted those contributions? |
| Retained and revised conclusions | What did I accept or reject, and why? |
| Next step | Which unknowns remain worth investigating? |

If developed into a tool, this should not merely reward the number of entries. Completing many fields does not demonstrate deep thought. The reasons must still withstand examination, and new evidence must be able to change the judgment.

## Put Slowness Where It Matters

I do not argue that every spelling correction or formatting task requires a prior analysis, or that emergencies should be delayed by extra reflection steps added to existing response procedures.

My concern is with stages involving understanding, trade-offs, and responsibility. Preserving judgment there does not require people to perform every operation. It requires them to explain how a question was formed, how evidence was used, and why a conclusion was accepted.

In education, this means considering students' understanding of their choices alongside the final work. In research, it means retaining question revisions and source checks. In public decisions, it also requires authority, review, and responsibility to be specified.

## Learn to Ask Better Questions

"Do not rush to obtain answers; first learn to ask better questions" increasingly feels like a concrete research requirement: before accepting an answer, examine whether it addresses a question worth asking, answerable with the available evidence, and useful for action.

AI can help me explore explanations more quickly. Research often advances, however, when I discover that the original question was too broad, a condition remained unspecified, or a confident conclusion still lacked evidence.

I want to preserve the ability to revise the question. A good research process can end with clearer unknowns rather than erase uncertainty to produce a complete-looking conclusion.
