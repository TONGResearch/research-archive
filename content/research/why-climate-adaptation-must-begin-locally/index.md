Discussions of climate change often begin with global average temperatures, national emissions targets, and energy systems. These scales matter. A subdistrict official, however, must also ask which locations need inspection, which residents may be unable to move to safety independently, and which infrastructure failures would disrupt community life.

While developing my course paper, I gradually understood Professor Guttman's emphasis on local focus. Narrowing the scale did not make the problem simple. Instead, it revealed differences that averages had concealed.

## What Makes Adaptation Local?

The comparative adaptation report used in the course emphasises both the place-specific character of adaptation and the need for coordination across jurisdictions. This encouraged me to understand "local" as an analytical requirement, not merely a category in an administrative hierarchy. [Report and download](https://napawash.org/news/adapting-to-the-impacts-of-climate-change-a-comparative-study-of-governance-processes-in-australia-china-and-the-united-states).

A river can cross administrative boundaries. An infrastructure failure can exceed the capacity of a single subdistrict. Beginning locally does not mean expecting local institutions to solve everything alone.

It means studying consequences closely enough to understand them while tracing their connections to larger systems.

## Similar Weather Does Not Necessarily Mean Similar Losses

Weather conditions provide only part of the information needed for risk analysis. The IPCC's account of risk stresses interactions among climate-related hazards, exposure, and vulnerability. Rainfall or temperature alone cannot tell us who will be affected or how. [IPCC glossary](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/annex-ii/).

Consider two hypothetical communities exposed to similar rainfall. One occupies higher ground and has well-maintained drainage. The other has more underground spaces and some residents with limited mobility. This is an analytical illustration, not a survey finding about actual communities, but it shows why comparable weather need not produce comparable consequences.

For public decisions, the useful question is how weather conditions interact with buildings, roads, and living conditions to create particular risks.

## Finer Data Can Still Miss Important Conditions

When preparing my Taiyuan adaptation policy memo, I became interested in the distance between administrative information and knowledge from the field. A map can show a road without indicating whether construction has blocked an entrance. Population statistics can describe age distributions without identifying who needs assistance on a particular day.

This does not make formal data unimportant. Rapidly changing conditions make reliable recording, updating, and communication more important, not less.

James C. Scott's discussion of legibility and simplification in *Seeing Like a State* offered a way to think about this problem. Organising action requires making complex situations readable, but those representations may omit relationships and distinctions embedded in local practice. [Original introduction](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.tufts.edu/dist/a/5820/files/2020/09/Scott-Seeing-Intro.pdf).

My research implication is to examine what simplification preserves and create routes through which omitted information can return to the decision process. This is my application of Scott's argument, not a claim he made directly about AI systems.

## How Might Local Knowledge Enter a System?

Incorporating residents' experience need not mean turning every observation into another score. For a risk report, retaining its source, time, location, and review status may matter more than immediately assigning a precise numerical weight.

Reports can also conflict. A system should preserve disagreement and support verification rather than assuming the newest report automatically supersedes all previous observations.

Digital reporting must not become the only route into the process. People unable to use an application need ways to communicate through staff, telephone, or in-person channels. Otherwise, a system may become better at seeing active reporters than at seeing people who most need support.

## How Local Focus Changed My Questions

I initially asked whether AI could help governments respond to climate change. I now prefer to begin by asking whose work it could support, which information it could organise for a defined risk, how field knowledge would correct its conclusions, and how errors would be found.

These are smaller questions, but questions for which evidence is more attainable. They also change how I understand computing's contribution: not only processing more information, but making information traceable, correctable, and usable in action.

My current view is that beginning locally helps research encounter actual consequences and then identify the resources and coordination needed at higher levels.
